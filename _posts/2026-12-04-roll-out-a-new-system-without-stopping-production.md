Ask many shop owners why they have not improved their systems, and the answer is the same: "We can't stop production." It is a fair concern. A shop floor that stops is a shop that loses money.

The good news is that you do not have to stop. A well-planned rollout runs beside live work, one small piece at a time. This guide explains how.

## Why big-bang rollouts fail
The traditional way to introduce a new system is to pick a date, switch off the old method, and switch on the new one. This is called a big-bang rollout. It sounds efficient, but it carries heavy risk.

If anything goes wrong, the whole shop is affected at once. Staff are learning the new way while still trying to ship orders. Mistakes are costly, frustration is high, and people often retreat to the old method in the middle of the chaos.

A staged rollout avoids these problems by keeping the risk small. Instead of changing everything at once, you change one thing, prove it works, and then expand.

## The principle: the old way keeps running
The key idea is that the old process stays in place until the new one has earned trust. If the new quoting tool has a problem, the quotes still go out the old way. If the new job tracking has a gap, the paper cards still exist as a safety net.

This takes pressure off everyone. People can try the new way knowing that a mistake will not stop the shop. And you gain real evidence about what works before committing fully.

### Step 1: Pick one painful step
Do not start with the whole shop. Choose one specific, painful step in your workflow. Common choices include quoting, job status tracking, or the handover between departments.
Pick something where the pain is clear and the benefit will be visible. A good first target has a small number of users, a clear measure of success, and low risk if it needs adjusting.

### Step 2: Run the new way on one job
Take one real job, or a small group of jobs, and run them through the new process while the old process continues for everything else. Watch what happens. Where do people get confused? What takes longer than expected? What works better than you hoped?
Gather feedback from the people doing the work. They will spot problems you cannot see from the office. Fix those issues before moving on.

### Step 3: Expand only when it works
Once the new way works reliably on one job, add a few more. Then a whole job type. Then another team. Expand at a pace that matches your confidence, not a calendar date.
At each stage, keep the fallback available. Only retire the old method when the new one has proven itself across enough cases that you trust it. This is slower than a big bang on paper, but it is usually faster in practice because you avoid rework and recovery.

## What to prepare before you start
- A clear statement of the problem and how you will measure improvement.
- A named owner for the rollout, even if it is part of their job.
- Time set aside for the people involved to learn the new way.
- A simple way to collect feedback, such as a short chat at the end of each week.

## Common mistakes
- Trying to fix too many things at once.
- Skipping the feedback from the shop floor.
- Switching off the old process too early.
- Forgetting to tell customers or suppliers if the change affects them.

## What success looks like
A good rollout is almost boring. Production continues. A few people notice a task is easier. The measure you chose, such as quote time or job tracking accuracy, improves quietly. Eventually the old method fades away because nobody needs it.

That is the aim. Not a dramatic launch day, but a steady shift that the shop barely feels.

## A simple example of a staged rollout
Suppose you want better job status tracking. In week one, one supervisor uses the new method for a single job type, while the paper cards continue as normal. In week three, two more job types join. By week six the new method covers most work, and the paper cards are used only as a backup. At no point did anyone stop production, and each stage produced feedback that made the next stage easier.

## Frequently asked questions
**How long should a staged rollout take?**

It depends on the process, but a single step, such as quoting or job status, can often be trialled within a few weeks and expanded steadily after that.
