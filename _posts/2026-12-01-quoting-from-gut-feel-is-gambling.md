Experienced owners are proud of their instincts. They look at a drawing and know roughly what it will cost. Often they are right. That skill is real, and it took years to build.

But when an entire quoting process rests on gut feel, the business is taking a gamble. Sometimes it pays off. Sometimes it quietly costs thousands. This article explains why, and what to do about it.

## The consistency test
Here is a simple experiment. Take a part you have quoted before and quote it again today, without looking at the old quote. Then compare the two numbers.

Now try a harder version. Ask a second person in your shop to quote the same part. Compare their number with yours.

If the figures are close, your process is consistent. If they differ by a lot, you have found a problem. Two prices for the same job cannot both be right. In my experience, shops that rely on gut feel often see gaps like this, and they only find out when a job is won and runs over, or lost because it was priced too high.

## Why gut feel drifts
Gut feel is shaped by recent experience. If the last few jobs went smoothly, you may price the next one optimistically. If a recent job went badly, you may add a big safety margin and lose the quote. Mood, workload and the customer's reputation can nudge the number too.

None of this is a character flaw. It is how human judgement works. The risk is that nobody notices, because the quote goes out, the job is won or lost, and the memory fades.

## The hidden risks of one-person quoting
Many shops depend on one person to price jobs, usually the owner. That creates risks beyond accuracy.
- Bottleneck. Quotes wait until that person is free.
- Key-person risk. If they are sick, away or leave, the pricing knowledge goes with them.
- No training path. New staff cannot learn the method because it lives in someone's head.
- No audit trail. You cannot see why a number was chosen.

## The cure is already in your files
The good news is that you do not need to replace your instincts. You need to support them with evidence. Your past jobs already contain the answers. You have made similar parts before, and the real hours and material costs are recorded somewhere, even if they are buried in old folders.

Pull those results together and use them as the starting point for new quotes. Your judgement then adjusts the number for what is different, rather than inventing it from nothing. This keeps the benefit of experience and removes much of the randomness.

## What to ask yourself
- Would my quote for the same part match last year's?
- Would a new hire quote it the same way?
- Do I know how my quotes compare with actual results?
- What happens to my quoting if I am away for two weeks?
- How many of my past jobs could guide a quote today if I could find them in minutes?

## A better balance
The ideal is not a robot that ignores experience. It is a process where data does the heavy lifting and experience does the fine-tuning. You look at similar past jobs, see the actual hours, and then ask: what is different this time? A tighter tolerance? A new customer? A tricky material? That is where your instinct earns its keep.

## Getting started without software
Pick your five most common job types. For each, record the actual hours and material from the last few jobs. Keep the figures on one page and share it with whoever helps you quote. Use it for the next ten quotes, and then compare your quotes with actual results.
You will probably find places where your gut was spot on, and a few where it was off. Both are useful.

## A practical test for your team
Pick one real drawing and ask everyone who quotes in your business to price it separately, without discussing it. Put the numbers side by side. If they are close, you have a consistent process. If they are far apart, you have found a gap worth closing. Use the difference as a starting point for a conversation about what each person assumed, and write down the shared answer.

## Frequently asked questions
**Is experience-based quoting always wrong?**

No. Experience is valuable, especially for adjusting a number for what is different about a job. The risk comes when it is the only input. Pair it with real history for the best result.

**How many past jobs do I need?**

Two or three recent examples per job type is enough to start. The library improves as you add more completed jobs over time.
