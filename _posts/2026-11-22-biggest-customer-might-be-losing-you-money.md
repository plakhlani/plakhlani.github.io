Revenue feels good. A large order lands, the schedule fills up, and the shop is busy. It is easy to assume that the customer sending the most work is also the one making you the most money. That assumption is worth challenging.

In many manufacturing businesses, the biggest customer by revenue is not the biggest by profit. Sometimes it is not even profitable. The reason is simple, and it is not about bad pricing on day one. It is about what happens after the price is agreed.


## The slow leak inside a good account

Big customers tend to collect small concessions. A rush request comes in, and you say yes because you want to keep them happy. A part comes back with a minor issue, and you remake it at your cost. A discount is offered to protect the relationship before a contract renewal.

Each concession is small enough to justify. None of them feels like a decision. But together they act like a leak in a tank. The tank looks full from the top, and the level keeps dropping.

The invoice does not help. It shows the price you charged, not the hours that went into the job. A $50,000 customer who needs $12,000 of unlogged rework and overtime can earn you less than a $30,000 customer who needs none. That example is an illustration, not a statistic, but you can test it in your own books.

## Why owners miss it

There are three common reasons.
- Costs are scattered. Overtime is in payroll, remakes are in someone's head, and rush freight is in a supplier invoice.
- Month-end figures are blended. Profit is reported for the whole business, not per customer, so a weak account is hidden by a strong one.
- The relationship feels valuable. A big name on your customer list brings confidence, referrals and a sense of security. Those are real benefits, but they are not margin.

## The proof is in five jobs

You do not need a full costing system to test this. Take your top customer and pull their last five jobs. Add up the hours worked, the remakes, and any overtime or rush costs. Compare that with what you quoted.

If the numbers match, great. You have a healthy account and evidence to back it up. If the real cost is much higher, you have found money that was slipping away every month.

## Questions worth asking

Take a minute with these before you read on.
- you know your top customer's real margin, or only their revenue?
- When did you last change their pricing, and why?
- How many rush jobs did they send last quarter, and did you charge for them?
- Would you keep them on the current terms if the numbers said they were barely profitable?
- Which smaller customer would you grow if you had the capacity?

If you struggle to answer the first one, that is the real finding. It means your business is making decisions without the most important number.

## What a profitable customer looks like

A profitable customer is not always the biggest. Often it is the one with clear drawings, realistic lead times, fair terms and few surprises. Their jobs run close to the quote. Their payments arrive on time. They make your shop more predictable.

Once you know which customers fit that description, you can plan around them. You can price rush work honestly, say no to jobs that stretch your team, and spend your sales effort on accounts that actually lift the bottom line.

## What changes when you can see the numbers

Owners who can see margin per customer stop guessing. Pricing conversations become factual. A customer asking for a discount meets an owner who knows what the job really costs. Rush work gets a rush price. Remakes get a reason code and a fix.

None of this requires being harsh. It requires being informed. You can still choose to keep a thin-margin customer for strategic reasons. The difference is that you will be choosing with your eyes open.

## Next step

## A fair word on strategic customers

Some owners keep a thin-margin customer on purpose. The name brings credibility, the volume keeps machines running, or the relationship opens doors elsewhere. That can be a sound decision. The point is not to drop every low-margin account. The point is to know which ones they are, decide deliberately, and set a limit on how much extra work you will absorb for free.

## Frequently asked questions

**Should I drop a customer with a thin margin?**
Not automatically. First try to fix the terms: charge for rush work, limit free fixes and review the price. Drop a customer only if the numbers stay poor after you have tried.
