Rework is one of the most expensive things in a manufacturing shop, and one of the least visible. A part fails inspection, gets remade, and ships a day late. Everyone moves on. The invoice shows the same price it always did, so the job looks fine on paper.

The cost, though, is real. It is paid in overtime, in machine time that could have run something else, and in material that went into the bin. If nobody records it, nobody can fix it. This guide shows a simple way to start tracking rework today with a pen and a job card.

## Why rework stays hidden

Rework hides because it does not belong to any single system. The remade part uses the same job number. The extra hours get lumped into the original timesheet. The reason for the failure lives in the operator's memory, or in a conversation across the shop floor.

There is also a social side. Nobody wants to log their own mistake, especially if the record might be used against them. If tracking feels like blame, people stop doing it. The way you introduce it matters as much as the form you use.

The result is a shop that repeats the same errors. A wrong setup, a misread drawing or a worn tool produces remakes month after month, and the owner only sees the effect as a vague sense that jobs take longer than they should.

### Step 1: Add a remake tick-box to every job card
Start with the lowest-effort change you can make. Add a simple tick-box labelled "Remake" to your job card, whether it is paper or digital. If a job or part is remade, someone ticks it.

This single tick answers the first question you cannot currently answer: how many jobs were remade last month? You do not need to know why yet. You just need to know how often.

Keep it quick. If ticking the box takes more than a few seconds, it will not get done. Put it where the operator already writes, such as the end-of-job sign-off.

### Step 2: Write the reason in one word
Next to the tick-box, add space for a one-word reason. Keep the list short and familiar to your team. Examples might include:
- Setup
- Tooling
- Programming
- Material
- Drawing
- Measurement

Resist the urge to build a long category list. A one-word answer that is written 90 per cent of the time is far more useful than a detailed form that is skipped. You can add detail later when the habit is established.

### Step 3: Review the top three reasons every Friday
Set aside ten minutes each Friday. Count the remakes for the week and look at the top three reasons. Do not chase every remake. Look for patterns.

If "Drawing" shows up four times, the problem may be unclear information from a customer, or a revision control issue in your own shop. If "Setup" leads, you may need a better setup sheet or training. If "Tooling" repeats on one machine, the answer may be a maintenance schedule.

Pick one cause and fix it. Then watch whether that reason drops over the following weeks. This is how tracking turns into improvement.

## Put a cost on it
Once you have a few weeks of data, put a rough cost against each remake. Count the extra hours, the lost material, and the machine time. Even an estimate helps. It changes the conversation from "we had a few remakes" to "remakes cost us around this much a month".

That figure is also useful when talking to customers. If one customer's drawings drive most of your remakes, you now have evidence for a conversation about clearer information or a charge for changes.

## Common mistakes to avoid
- Making the form too long, so it is skipped.
- Using the data to blame people, so the numbers dry up.
- Collecting data but never reviewing it.
- Fixing every issue at once instead of the top one.

## When paper stops working

How you launch this matters. Tell your operators plainly that the goal is to find causes, not to blame people. Show them the first Friday review and let them suggest fixes. When operators see a recurring problem get solved because they logged it, they trust the process, and the data keeps flowing. If the first thing people see is a reprimand, the tick-box will quietly stop being used.

A paper tick-box is a good start, but it has limits. Someone has to type the data into a spreadsheet, and over time the sheet falls behind. Once you want to see remakes by customer, machine or part family, a simple custom system that captures the entry once, where the operator already works, makes the whole process lighter.
