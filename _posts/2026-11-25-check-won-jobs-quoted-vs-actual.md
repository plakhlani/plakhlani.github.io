Ask a manufacturer about their quoting, and they will usually talk about the ones they lost. Why was the competitor cheaper? Was the lead time too long? It feels natural to study losses.

Far fewer owners study the quotes they won. Yet the won jobs are where your profit, or your losses, actually live. A quote you win at the wrong price is worse than a quote you lose. This guide shows a simple way to check.


## Why won quotes need a second look
Underpriced jobs are the easiest to win. If your price is lower than the market expects, buyers say yes quickly. That feels like success. You hit your win-rate target, the schedule fills, and nobody questions the price.

The trouble is that you only find out later, when the job runs over. By then you have delivered it, invoiced it, and moved on. Unless you compare the result against the quote, the lesson is lost, and the next similar quote repeats the same mistake.

This is why the habit of checking won jobs is so valuable. It closes the loop between what you estimated and what actually happened.

### Step 1: List your last ten won jobs
Open your records and list the last ten jobs you won and completed. Aim for a mix of customers and job types if you can. Ten is enough to start seeing patterns without drowning in data.

### Step 2: Put quoted hours next to actual hours
For each job, record two numbers. First, the hours you quoted. Second, the hours the job actually took. If you also track material, record quoted versus actual material cost.

Where you do not have exact hours, use timesheets, job cards or your best estimate. Mark any guesses so you know how firm the numbers are.

Then work out the difference. A simple way is to divide actual hours by quoted hours. A result of 1.0 means you were exactly on target. A result of 1.3 means the job took about 30 per cent longer than you quoted.

### Step 3: Add margin to the job types that run over
Now sort the jobs by type. Look for patterns. Maybe your simple machined parts come in close to the quote, but your multi-step fabrication jobs regularly run 20 or 30 per cent over. That tells you something specific about your estimating.

Where a job type consistently runs over, adjust. You might add a margin to that type of quote, review how you estimate setup time, or ask for more information before pricing. Small changes, applied to the right job types, can move your margin without losing the customers who value your work.

## What a high win rate might mean
A high win rate is often treated as a sign of strength. It can be. But if you win almost every quote you send, ask whether your prices are too low. In a healthy market you should lose some jobs on price.

That does not mean you should chase a low win rate. It means you should look at the quality of the jobs you win, not only the quantity. A lower win rate with healthier margins is often a better business.

## Questions to ask yourself
- What is my win rate, and what do I think it should be?
- On the jobs I won, what was the average difference between quoted and actual hours?
- Which job types cause the biggest overruns?
- Do my estimators ever see the actual results of their quotes?
The last question matters. Estimators who never see actual results cannot improve. Feedback is the fastest way to sharpen a quote.

## Make it a habit
### A short worked example
Suppose you quoted 20 hours for a fabrication job and it took 26. Divide 26 by 20 and you get 1.3, a 30 per cent overrun. If three of your last four fabrication jobs show a similar ratio, you have a clear signal: either setup time is underestimated or something in that job type is not being priced. You can then test a higher allowance on the next quote and see whether you still win the work.

Do this check each quarter. It takes about an hour once you have the data in one place, and it can pay for itself on the first adjustment. Over time, your quotes get closer to reality and your pricing fixes itself.
