When a quote is lost, owners usually say the competitor was cheaper. Sometimes that is true. But often the real cause is timing. The other shop replied sooner, and by the time your quote arrived, the buyer had already moved on.

This guide explains why speed matters so much in manufacturing quotes, and gives you a three-step method to find and fix the slowest part of your process.

## Why timing beats price more often than you think
When a buyer needs a part, they rarely contact only one shop. They send the same drawing to several, then wait. The first reply does something powerful. It sets the price expectation in the buyer's mind. Every later quote is compared against it.

The first reply also shows how organised and responsive you are. A buyer who is busy and under pressure often leans toward the supplier who made their life easier by answering quickly. A quote that takes two weeks suggests that the job itself may be slow and difficult to manage.

This is why a 15-day quote is, in practice, a rejected quote. It may be accurate and well priced. It simply arrives after the decision has already been made.

## Where the time actually goes
Most owners assume pricing is the slow part. In many shops, it is not. The pricing itself might take an hour. The days are lost elsewhere.
- Waiting for information. Missing drawings, unclear requirements, or a question that sits unanswered in someone's inbox.
- Hunting for data. Looking for last year's price, an old spreadsheet or a supplier's current cost.
- Waiting for a person. The quote needs the owner's approval, and the owner is on the floor or on the road.
- Rebuilding from scratch. Each quote starts with a blank sheet, even for a part you have made before.

### Step 1: Log how many days each quote takes
You cannot fix what you cannot see. For the next two weeks, record three dates for every quote: when the enquiry arrived, when work on the quote began, and when the quote was sent. A simple notebook or spreadsheet is enough.

At the end of two weeks, you will have a clear picture of your real turnaround time. Many owners are surprised. The quote they think takes two days often takes six, because it sits untouched in a queue.

### Step 2: Find the slowest step
Now look at where the days go. Is it the gap between the enquiry and the first look? Is it waiting on a supplier price? Is it the final approval?

Mark each stage and note how long quotes typically wait there. One stage will usually stand out. That is your bottleneck. Everything else is a smaller problem, and working on it first would be wasted effort.

### Step 3: Fix that one step first
Choose a fix that matches the bottleneck.
- If information is missing, create a short checklist of what you need before you start pricing, and send it back to the buyer straight away.
- If supplier prices slow you down, keep a current price list for your most-used materials.
- If approvals are the delay, agree a rule: quotes under a certain value can go out without owner sign-off.
- If every quote starts from scratch, build templates and use past jobs as starting points.
Fix one thing, measure again, and then move to the next. Small steady improvements add up quickly.

## What speed does for the whole business
Faster quoting does more than win jobs. It frees up the owner's time, reduces the pile of half-finished quotes in the background, and gives you a clearer view of what is coming into the shop. It also tends to improve accuracy, because a repeatable process leaves less room for mistakes.

## A realistic target
You do not need to reply in minutes for every job. A complex, one-off part may need real thought. But for repeat or similar work, a same-day or next-day reply is a realistic goal, and it is often enough to move you to the front of the queue.

## A small example
Imagine a quote that takes ten days. Two of those days are spent actually pricing. Three are spent waiting for a supplier price. Three more are spent waiting for the owner to approve it. The last two are spent finding the drawing revision. Pricing was never the problem. Once you see that split, the fix is obvious: keep supplier prices current and let trusted staff approve routine quotes. The pricing time barely changes, but the total drops sharply.
