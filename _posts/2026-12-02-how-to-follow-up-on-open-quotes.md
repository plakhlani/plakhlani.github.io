You send the quote. Then the phone rings, a machine stops, or a customer arrives, and the quote slips out of mind. A week later, you wonder what happened to it. Nobody replied, so you assume the buyer went elsewhere.

Sometimes that is true. But often the quote was never lost on price. It was simply forgotten, by you and by the buyer. A short, well-timed follow-up could have turned it into a job. This guide shows a simple way to follow up on every open quote without buying a CRM.

## Why quotes go quiet
Buyers are busy too. They send enquiries to several shops, receive a handful of replies, and then get pulled into other tasks. A quote that arrives without a nudge can sink to the bottom of an inbox.

On your side, the owner is often on the floor all day. Follow-up happens on the drive home, if at all. There is no list of open quotes, no reminder, and no habit. The process depends on memory, and memory is unreliable when the shop is busy.

The result is a pile of quotes in limbo. Some would have been won with a single call. Others were lost long ago and should be closed off so they stop cluttering your picture of the pipeline.

## Why follow-up is worth the effort
Following up costs almost nothing. It takes a few minutes per quote. Compared with the effort of winning a brand-new customer, nudging an existing enquiry is cheap. It also shows that you are reliable, which buyers notice.

There is another benefit. A follow-up call often surfaces useful information. The buyer may say the project was delayed, the budget changed, or a competitor was cheaper. Each answer tells you something about your market and your pricing.

### Step 1: List every open quote
Start with a single list of every quote you have sent that has not been accepted or declined. Include the customer, the date sent, the value, and the contact person. If you do not have a tidy source, go through your sent emails and job folders.

This list alone is useful. Many owners are surprised by how many quotes are hanging, and by how much value is attached to them. Add up the total and you may find it is larger than you expected.

### Step 2: Set a follow-up date for each
Next to each quote, write a follow-up date. A common rule is to follow up two to three days after sending, then again a week later. Adjust for the size of the job. Larger quotes with longer decision cycles may need a slower rhythm.

Put the dates somewhere you will see them, such as a calendar, a spreadsheet you open every morning, or a whiteboard in the office. The system matters less than the habit of looking at it.

### Step 3: Call or email each one this week
Now work through the list. For each quote, send a short, friendly message. Keep it simple.
- Mention the quote by name or number.
- Ask if they have any questions about the price or scope.
- Ask whether the timing has changed.
- Offer to adjust the quote if the requirements have changed.
Avoid pressure. The goal is to be helpful and present. A phone call often works better than an email for larger jobs, because it invites a quick answer.

## Close the loop
Every quote should end in one of three states: won, lost, or closed with a reason. If a buyer says no, ask politely why. Record the reason, whether it was price, timing, or something else. Over time those notes become a map of your market.
Common mistakes
- Following up once and giving up.
- Writing long messages that are easy to ignore.
- Sounding desperate instead of helpful.
- Never updating the list, so it fills with dead quotes.

## A simple weekly routine
Put ten minutes on the calendar every Monday morning. Review the list, check the follow-up dates, and send the day's messages. If you do this consistently, you will recover jobs you would otherwise have lost to silence, and you will have a clear picture of your pipeline at all times.

## When a list is not enough
### A sample follow-up message
Here is a short message you can adapt: "Hi, I wanted to check in on the quote I sent on the 12th for the bracket assembly. Is there anything I can clarify on the price or lead time? If your requirements have changed, I am happy to update it." It is polite, specific and easy to answer. Short messages like this tend to get replies because they take only a few seconds to read.

A manual list works for a while. As the number of quotes grows, it becomes harder to keep up. That is when a simple custom system helps, one that tracks every quote, shows what is due for follow-up, and reminds you automatically, built around how you already work.
