When a shop has a quality problem, the usual response is to push harder on the floor. More checks, more reminders, a stern talk at the Monday meeting. Sometimes it works for a week. Then the same errors come back.

The reason is that many quality problems are not really quality problems. They are tracking problems. If you cannot see what is going wrong, how often and why, you cannot fix it. You can only react.

## A simple test
Try this. How many jobs were remade in your shop last month? Now ask a harder question. Where is that number written down?

If you do not know, or if the answer lives in one person's memory, then you cannot claim that quality is improving. You may be improving. You may be getting worse. You are guessing, and so is everyone else in the building.

That is the proof. Improvement needs a baseline. Without a baseline, every claim is an opinion.

## What untracked rework looks like
In shops with weak tracking, you tend to see the same patterns.
- The same errors repeat on different jobs because nobody connected them.
- Remakes are handled by shouting across the floor, then forgotten.
- Overtime appears at the end of the week with no clear explanation.
- Customer complaints feel random, because there is no record to show a trend.
- Everyone has a theory about the cause, and nobody has data.
None of this means your people are careless. It means the shop has no memory. Each remake is treated as a one-off event instead of one more data point in a pattern.

## Why "try harder" does not work
Pushing for better quality without data is like trying to lose weight without ever stepping on a scale. Effort is real, but direction is missing. You might work hard on the wrong cause.

For example, you might retrain operators on a skill when the true cause is a worn fixture. Or you might blame a supplier when most remakes come from unclear drawing revisions. Without records, you will not know until you have spent time and goodwill on the wrong fix.

Tracking also changes behaviour in a good way. When people know a remake is counted and reviewed calmly, without blame, they start to spot causes themselves. The conversation moves from "who did this" to "what is causing this".

## The cheapest first step
You do not need a new system to start. A tick-box on the job card for "remake", plus a one-word reason, gives you a count and a cause. A ten-minute review each Friday turns that into action.

The key is consistency. Ten weeks of simple data beats one perfect report that was built once and never updated.

## Questions worth sitting with
- How many remakes did you have last month?
- Who knows that number, and where is it written?
- What are the top three causes, and how do you know?
- If a customer asked for your remake rate tomorrow, could you give it to them?
- If you fixed one cause this quarter, which would it be?
If these are hard to answer, you have found your real quality problem. It sits a step before the machines and the people.

## What changes when you track
Once remakes are visible, three things tend to happen. You find that a few causes account for most of the waste, which makes the fixes manageable. You can put a rough cost on rework, which gives you a reason to act. And you can show customers and auditors evidence that you manage quality, rather than hoping you do.

There is also a quieter benefit. Owners stop carrying it all in their heads. The shop starts to remember its own mistakes, so it can stop repeating them.

## Beyond the job card

### A small example
Imagine a shop that remakes parts on a certain machine every few weeks. The team assumes the operators are rushing. After a month of tracking, the reasons show "Tooling" most often, all on one machine. The fix is a revised tool-change schedule, not a lecture. Without the record, that pattern would have stayed invisible, and the wrong people would have been blamed.

## Frequently asked questions
**How long before tracking shows results?**
Usually a few weeks. A month of consistent tick-box data is often enough to reveal the top two or three causes of rework, which gives you something concrete to fix.
A paper tick-box is a start. As the shop grows, you will want remakes tied to customers, machines and part types, and you will want the data entered once, in the place where the operator already works. That is where a custom system built around your process pays off.
