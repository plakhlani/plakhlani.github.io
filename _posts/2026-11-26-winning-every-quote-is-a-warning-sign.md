There is a moment every quoting owner enjoys. The email arrives and says the job is yours. It feels like proof that you are good at what you do. Do it often enough and you start to treat a high win rate as the main measure of success.

Here is the uncomfortable thought. If you win nearly every quote you send, your prices may be lower than the market is willing to pay. A win is only a win if the job makes money. Otherwise it is just work.

## The logic of a very high win rate

In a normal market, buyers compare several suppliers. Some choose on price, some on speed, some on trust. If you are priced fairly, you should lose some quotes. A few buyers will find someone cheaper, and a few will pick a closer shop. That is healthy.

If you almost never lose, it suggests that your price sits below what most buyers expected to pay. They say yes immediately because the number is easy to accept. That is not a sign of brilliant selling. It is often a sign of leaving money on the table.

Think of it like selling a car. If every buyer who looks at it buys it within an hour, you probably priced it too low. A few buyers walking away would tell you that you were close to the right number.

## Why owners do not question it
There are a few understandable reasons.
- A full schedule feels safe. When the shop is busy, it is easy to assume the pricing is fine.
- Nobody checks the results. Quotes go out, jobs get done, and rarely does anyone compare the quoted hours with the actual hours.
- Losing a quote hurts more than winning an underpriced one. A loss is visible and annoying. A thin-margin win is invisible until the month-end numbers arrive.
- Pricing is based on feel. If quotes come from gut instinct or from last year's price, there is no data to challenge them.

## The proof you can check this week
You can test this without any new tools. Take your last ten won jobs. For each, write the hours you quoted next to the hours the job actually took. Do the same for material cost if you can.

If most jobs came in close to the quote, your pricing is probably sound, and a high win rate may simply mean you are good at what you do. If many jobs ran over, your win rate may be hiding a margin problem. Underpriced jobs win easily, which is exactly why you keep winning them.

## What a healthier picture looks like
A healthy shop usually knows three things. It knows its win rate. It knows its average margin on the jobs it wins. And it knows which job types consistently run over or under the quote.

With those three numbers, you can make deliberate choices. You might raise prices on job types that run over and see how the win rate responds. A small drop in win rate paired with a clear gain in margin is often a good trade.

You might also choose to hold your price on work you are especially good at, and be more aggressive on jobs that fill gaps in the schedule. That is strategy, not guesswork.

## Questions to ask yourself
- What is my win rate over the last six months?
- Is a high win rate a good sign for my business, or a warning?
- How many of my won jobs ran over the quoted hours?
- When did I last raise my prices, and what happened to my win rate?
- Who reviews quotes against actual results, and how often?

## A note on fear
Many owners are afraid to raise prices because they fear losing customers. That fear is understandable, but it is often based on a feeling, not evidence. Small, targeted increases on the right jobs, tested one at a time, let you learn quickly with limited risk. If a customer leaves over a modest increase, you have learned something about the relationship. If nobody leaves, you have found easy margin.

## Close the loop
### A quick self-check
Write down your win rate for the last six months, then your average margin on the jobs you won. If both are high, you are in a strong position. If the win rate is high and the margin is thin, your prices are probably doing the winning for you. Repeat the check each quarter and watch how the two numbers move together.
The core fix is simple. Compare quote to result on every job you win, and use what you learn in the next quote. When that comparison is easy to see, pricing improves almost automatically.
