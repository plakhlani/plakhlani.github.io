Every time you start a quote from a blank page, you throw away something valuable. You have made similar parts before. You know roughly how long they take and what material they use. But that knowledge is usually buried in old job files, old emails and the memory of whoever did the work.

A quote library turns that history into a starting point for new quotes. It saves time, improves consistency, and means the quote does not depend on one person's memory. This guide shows how to build a simple one.

## What a quote library is
A quote library is a collection of real, completed jobs, organised by type, with the actual hours and material costs recorded for each. When a new enquiry arrives, you find the closest match in the library and adjust it, instead of building from scratch.

It does not have to be fancy. A well-organised spreadsheet works to start. The value is in the data, not in the software.

## Why it works
Estimates based on real results are usually better than estimates based on guesses. If you have made fifty similar brackets, you know within a reasonable range how long the next one will take. Your gut feel may be good, but a written record is better, and it can be shared with the rest of the team.

It also helps with consistency. If two people quote the same part a month apart and get very different numbers, something is wrong. A shared library narrows the gap.

And it helps when someone new joins. A new estimator can learn from the history of the shop instead of from trial and error.

### Step 1: Pick your ten most repeated job types
Start small. Look back over the last year and find the types of work you do most often. These might be machined parts of a certain size, fabrication jobs such as frames or enclosures, or assemblies you build regularly.

Choose the ten that appear most often. These are the jobs where a library will pay off fastest, because you will use it again and again.

### Step 2: Record actual hours and material for each
For each job type, pull two or three recent examples and record what actually happened. Not what you quoted, but what the job took.
- Actual hours, split by stage where possible, such as programming, setup, machining and finishing.
- Actual material used, including waste.
- Any outsourced steps, such as coating or heat treatment.
- Notes about anything unusual that affected the job.
If you do not have exact figures, use the best information you can find from timesheets and job cards. Mark estimates so you know how reliable each record is.

### Step 3: Use them as the starting point for every new quote
When an enquiry arrives, ask: which library entry is closest? Start with that entry's hours and material. Then adjust for differences, such as a larger size, a tighter tolerance or a different material.

Over time you will add new entries and refine old ones. Each completed job is a chance to improve the library. That is how it gets more accurate every month.

## Keep it honest

A library is only useful if the data is true. A few habits help.
- Record actual hours, not planned hours.
- Update entries when a new job shows a different result.
- Note when a job was unusual so it does not distort the average.
- Review the library every quarter and remove outdated entries.

## Mistakes to avoid
- Building it too big. Ten job types is enough to begin. A giant library that never gets finished helps nobody.
- Mixing quoted and actual figures. Use actuals only.
- Keeping it in one person's head or laptop. Store it where the quoting team can reach it.
- Never updating it. A library that is two years old will mislead you.

## A short example
Imagine you have made a certain type of welded frame many times. Your library entry shows an average of 14 hours of fabrication and 3 hours of finishing, plus a typical material list. A new enquiry arrives for a frame that is slightly larger. You start with the library figures, add a margin for the extra size, and send the quote the same day. No digging through old folders, no guessing.

## Where it leads
### Frequently asked questions
**What if I have no records of actual hours?**
Start recording them now on every job. In the meantime, use timesheets and job cards from recent work to build rough entries, and mark them as estimates until real figures replace them.

Once a library is in place, you can go further. You can link it to a quoting template, show the quoted versus actual result on each job, and see which job types consistently run over. That is when quoting becomes a loop that improves itself.
