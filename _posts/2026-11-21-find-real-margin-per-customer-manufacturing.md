Most shop owners know which customer sends the biggest invoices. Far fewer know which customer leaves the most profit. The two are often not the same, and the gap can be large enough to change how you run the business.
This guide shows a simple way to find the real margin per customer using information you already have. You do not need new software. You need a pen, your last few job files, and about an hour.

## Why the biggest customer can be the least profitable

Large customers tend to get special treatment. They get rush priority when a job is late. They get a small discount to keep the relationship warm. They get free fixes when a part is slightly off. Each of these feels minor on the day. A few hours of overtime here, a quick remake there.

The problem is that none of it shows up on the invoice. The invoice shows the price. It does not show the extra labour, the machine time lost to a remake, or the weekend shift that got the job out the door. So the account looks healthy, and the margin quietly disappears.

Here is a simple example. Say Customer A pays $50,000 across five jobs, but those jobs needed $12,000 of unplanned rework and overtime. Customer B pays $30,000 across five jobs with no extra effort. On paper, A is the better customer. In your bank account, B may be leaving you more. These numbers are an illustration, not a benchmark, but the pattern is real in many shops.

### Step 1: Pick your top three customers

Start with the three customers who bring in the most revenue. These are the accounts you protect, the ones you rush for, and the ones you hesitate to push on price. They are also the accounts most likely to hide margin problems.

Write their names at the top of a page or a spreadsheet. Next to each, list their last five jobs. Five is enough to see a pattern without taking all afternoon.

### Step 2: Add up the hidden costs on their last five jobs

For each job, look for the costs that never made it onto the quote. Go through your job cards, emails and timesheets, and note the following.
- Hours worked beyond what you quoted, including overtime and weekend work.
- Remakes or rework, and how many hours they took.
- Rush charges you paid, such as express freight or a supplier premium.
- Discounts or free extras you agreed to on the day.
- Time spent chasing information, approvals or missing drawings.

If you do not have exact figures, estimate the hours and multiply by your internal hourly cost. A rough number is still more useful than no number. Mark estimates clearly so you remember which figures are firm.
### Step 3: Compare the real cost with what you quoted

Now put the two numbers side by side. What did you quote for these five jobs, and what did they really cost you once the hidden items are added? Subtract the real cost from the price you charged. That is your actual margin on that customer.

Repeat for the other two customers. You now have three real margins instead of three invoice totals. Most owners find at least one surprise. Sometimes the biggest account is thin. Sometimes a smaller customer who pays on time and never asks for favours is the quiet star.

## What to do with the result

Knowing the number gives you choices. You can adjust your pricing for that customer, charge for rush work, or set limits on free fixes. You can have a calm conversation about scope instead of absorbing extra work in silence. In some cases you may decide a customer is not worth keeping on current terms.

You can also learn which job types cause the overruns. If rework keeps showing up on one part family, the fix may be in your process, not in the customer.
Do not treat this as a one-off. Margins drift. A customer who was profitable two years ago can slide as their requests change. A quarterly check on your top accounts keeps surprises small.

## Making it easier next time

### A quick example you can copy

Here is a simple layout. Make four columns: customer, quoted price, real cost, margin. Fill in one row per customer for their last five jobs combined. If Customer A shows a quoted price of $50,000 and a real cost of $48,000, the margin is only $2,000, or about four per cent. If Customer B shows $30,000 against $22,000, the margin is $8,000, or about 27 per cent. These figures are only an illustration, but laying them out this way makes the comparison obvious in a few minutes.

The reason this check feels like a project is that the data is scattered. Hours sit in timesheets, remakes live in people's memories, and quotes are in old emails. If you record quoted hours, actual hours and remake reasons against each job as it happens, the customer margin report takes minutes instead of an afternoon.
