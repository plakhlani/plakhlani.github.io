When a new system fails to catch on, the usual story is that the team resisted change. Operators were stuck in their ways, or supervisors were too busy, or nobody took it seriously. The owner is left feeling that the problem is cultural.

I would like to offer a different view. If your team will not use the system, the system is wrong. Not the people. This is a claim worth arguing about, so here is the reasoning.

## The pattern I see again and again

A manufacturing business buys or builds a tool. It is chosen in the office, often by the owner or an office manager, based on features and price. It is rolled out with a briefing and a login. For a few weeks, there is enthusiasm. Then usage drops. Operators go back to paper, supervisors keep spreadsheets, and the new system fills with stale data.

The owner concludes that the shop floor is resistant. In truth, the system was designed for the person who bought it, not the people who had to use it every day.

## The proof: tools built for the office get ignored on the floor
Consider what a shop floor actually needs. Quick entry, minimal typing, clear status, and nothing that gets in the way of the machine. Consider what many tools offer. Long forms, many screens, features nobody asked for, and a design that assumes a desk and a quiet moment.

When the gap between the two is large, people choose the easy path. That is not stubbornness. It is rational behaviour. If the paper card takes ten seconds and the screen takes two minutes, the paper card wins.

## Who was asked?
A revealing question is this: who was asked before your last system was chosen? Was it the person using it every day? Or was it the person who pays for it?

Many failed projects have one thing in common. The people who will live with the system were consulted late, or not at all. They were shown the finished product and expected to adapt. By then, the design had hardened around assumptions that did not match the reality of the floor.

When operators help shape the system, three things change. The design fits their real tasks. They understand why it exists. And they feel some ownership, which makes them advocates instead of critics.

## Is it really that simple?
Honest disagreement is fair here. There are cases where people resist change for reasons that have nothing to do with design. Trust may be low, workloads may be unreasonable, or a previous change may have been mishandled. Leadership matters too. If the owner does not use the system or ask for its data, nobody else will take it seriously.

But even in those cases, design is the first thing to check, because it is the one thing you can fix quickly. A system that is hard to use will fail even in a willing team. A system that is easy to use has a good chance even in a sceptical one.

## Signs your system is the problem
- Operators say it takes longer than the old way.
- The same data has to be entered in more than one place.
- People keep unofficial sheets "just in case".
- Training took hours, and people still forget how to use it.
- Nobody can say who the system is for.
If three or more of these ring true, the design needs attention, not the attitude.

## What a system that gets used looks like
Systems that survive tend to share a few features. Data is entered once, where the work happens. The screens match the vocabulary and sequence the team already uses. The system returns value quickly, so operators see a benefit, such as fewer questions from the office or an easier handover. And someone owns it, responding to feedback and improving it over time.

## Questions to ask yourself
- Who was asked before my last system was chosen?
- Was it the person who uses it every day?
- Where do operators have to type the same thing twice?
- What do they say they would change first?

## Frequently asked questions
**Could the problem be the team after all?**

It can be, especially where trust is low or leadership does not use the data. But check the design first, because it is the quickest thing to fix and the most common cause.

**How do I find out why people avoid it?**

Watch them work for an hour. Note every point where they stop, retype or reach for paper. Those moments show you exactly where the design needs to change.
